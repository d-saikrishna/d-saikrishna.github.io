# Vagina Monologues | Play review
*This play review is originally written in Telugu. The following is an AI assisted English translation*

The play began with several disclaimers. “This play might make you uncomfortable. Please sit with that discomfort. Don’t walk away,” a woman announced at the beginning.

I became curious. What was this discomfort she was talking about?

By then, we already knew the play was called **The Vagina Monologues**. Periods, sex, rape, orgasms — we men had prepared ourselves for all of it. We are good men, after all. Surely, listening to women talk about these things wouldn’t make us too uncomfortable.

<div style="text-align: center"><img src="vagina_monologues_review/vagina_monologues.webp" width="50%"/> </div>

But we became uncomfortable right in the first scene. The dialogue hadn’t even begun.

About nine women lay down on the stage, raised their waists, and stretched out their legs. Imagine the Chakrasana yoga pose. With their faces tilted backwards and their legs stretched towards the audience, they held the pose, and for a while, we turned our faces away.

A question occurred to me. Why did we turn our faces away?

Was it our culture? Were we embarrassed to see women in that position in public? But, they were fully clothed while doing the pose. There was nothing we weren’t supposed to see that was actually visible. And yet, we turned our faces away. What was there in that pose that we needed to be so ashamed of? All they did was stretch out their legs, and our faces turned away.

Perhaps it was because we men rarely see women spreading their legs in public. Perhaps their clothes aren’t even designed to let them do so comfortably. Even men are told that sitting with our legs spread apart is not good manners. But women bear a greater responsibility for upholding these manners, so perhaps their clothes are made a little tighter still.

I have even read about the extreme lengths to which women’s clothing was designed to restrict them - Chastity belts. So, when legs that are supposed to remain so tightly contained suddenly open up in public, are we not supposed to feel ashamed? Perhaps that is why we turned our faces away.

<div style="text-align: center"><img src="vagina_monologues_review/actors.jpg" width="50%"/> </div>

The shame that began with the sight of those outstretched legs grew as the play went on. We had to confront all the things men had done in the name of “masculinity.”

Some of these moments were funny. For instance, we men, who casually hurl vulgar abuses at women by invoking their vaginas, realised how little we actually knew about them. That was embarrassing.

In one scene, the actors drew a picture of a vagina. Thank goodness they didn’t ask the audience to draw one. We would have sat there with blank faces.

Then the women began talking about their desires during sex. And we felt ashamed all over again. Had our masculinity, after all these years, failed even to fulfil these simple desires?

Well, what did we expect? Until now, it was men who had been telling us what women wanted. This was the first time we were hearing it directly from them. Perhaps this was better.

<div style="text-align: center"><img src="vagina_monologues_review/actors.JPG.jpeg" width="50%"/> </div>

The play also had moments that made the entire male population feel ashamed.

They openly showed us how a man rapes a woman. There was no man on stage. Just a woman acting out a man raping her. Perhaps that was why our attention went straight to her suffering.

When we watch rape scenes in films, the scene usually serves to make the hero angry and move the story forward. The suffering of the woman in that scene rarely reaches the audience in any meaningful way. In this play, it did.

We can argue that not all men are like that. Fair enough. But aren’t all women are in danger because of men? If we disagree, let us not be afraid when women in our own families step out at any hour of night. If we hesitate to do so, isn’t that something the entire male population should be ashamed of?

If we don’t want to feel ashamed, then perhaps nothing in this play should make us feel that way. Better still, perhaps there should be no need for a play like this at all.

Do women we know, women we can see just by turning our heads, really need to put on a play to tell us about their desires and fears?

Let us have these conversations with the women in our lives, without shame. Then, the next time we watch a play like this, we can sit there with our heads held high, our legs spread comfortably, and no need to turn our faces away.
